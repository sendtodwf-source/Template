GitHub is important for two big reasons:

It acts as a physical backup of your project that lives off your device (so if your computer crashes or gets lost, your work is still safe online).
It makes sharing and collaborating with others straightforward.

Because of this, it’s a really good habit to commit (and ideally push) every working version of your system as you go—so you always have clean restore points.
Here’s a concrete everyday example of version control in action:

Imagine you’re writing a school paper. You finish a solid draft on Monday and commit it (“Draft 1 – introduction and first two sections complete”). Coach: Great clarifying question, D the Student.
Commit creates a permanent snapshot of your staged changes on your own computer. It’s a local save point with a message. Nothing leaves your machine.
Push takes those commits you’ve already made and uploads them to the remote (usually GitHub). That’s when the backup and sharing happen.
So: commit = save locally → push = send the saved versions online.
Does that difference feel clear now?
D the Student: Why not just use ctrl+s
Coach: That’s an excellent question—lots of people wonder the same thing at first.
Ctrl+S (or the regular “Save” button) only updates the current version of the file on your computer. It overwrites the previous content, so you lose the earlier versions. There’s no history, no way to go back to last Tuesday’s working draft, no record of what changed or why, and no easy way for teammates to see or combine different people’s work.
Version control (git + commits) keeps every intentional save point you choose, so you can always restore an older working version, see the differences, and collaborate safely.
